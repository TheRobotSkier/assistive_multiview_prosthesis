\chapter{Testing Methodology and Evaluation} \label{ch:Testing}

To evaluate the feasibility and performance of the proposed solution, four tests were designed and conducted. Each test relates requirements from \autoref{sc:Requirements} to ensure logical progression from software validation to human trials.

The objective of these tests is to establish the system's capabilities while isolating specific variables systematically. The functional requirements are qualitatively assessed through pass/fail criteria during the procedures, while technical requirements are quantitatively measured against their defined marginal and ideal thresholds.

% ==========================================
% TEST 1: SOFTWARE VERIFICATION
% ==========================================
\section{Test 1: Software Verification}\label{sec:test1}

This offline test validates the core algorithms for point cloud processing, grasp planning, and intent prediction without the variability introduced by a human subject or physical hardware. By using a synthetic dataset, the computational limits of the software pipeline can be precisely measured, and the analytical advantage of the multi-view sensing system can be isolated against a known ground truth.

\subsection{Setup}
A synthetic dataset of point clouds is utilised, consisting of 9 distinct object models. Each object is rendered from two simulated viewpoints representing the head-mounted and prosthesis-mounted camera positions. The sensor framework configuration can be seen in \autoref{fig:synthetic_setup_3d}, while the geometric profiles of the synthetic objects used for grasping evaluation are detailed in \autoref{fig:object_gallery}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Latency Test (2.4 \& 1.6):} Simulated EMG triggers are injected into the pipeline. Code-level timestamps measure the duration from trigger input to the generation of the final grasp command. The 400\,ms marginal limit is established to accommodate the mechanical closing time of the MIA hand ($\sim$400\,ms) within a typical 800\,ms reach.
    \item \textbf{Occlusion Proxy Test (2.7):} The pipeline processes the dataset under two conditions: (a) Single-view, using only the simulated head-mounted camera frustum, and (b) Multi-view (MVSS), using both frustums. To establish a ground truth for the synthetic dataset, a High-Fidelity Baseline is generated by processing the unoccluded, full object point clouds through the SMC sampler with a significantly increased sample density (1 million samples). This serves as the ``Optimal Algorithmic Grasp,'' against which the real-time single-view and multi-view configurations are compared to calculate the Intent Precision $\Delta$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Results}
The offline software verification test was executed on a desktop workstation equipped with an Intel i5-13600K CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 4070 GPU.

\subsubsection{Pipeline Latency Evaluation (Req. 2.4)}
The pipeline latency was evaluated across 100 consecutive synthetic simulation runs to isolate computational bottlenecks and verify the impact of hardware acceleration. As illustrated in \autoref{fig:latency_waterfall}, transitioning the segmentation module from the CPU to an accelerated GPU backend yielded a significant latency reduction:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Base Configuration (CPU):} Exhibited a total processing latency of 955\,ms (consisting of 89\,ms twist propagation, 726\,ms segmentation, and 140\,ms grasp preshaping). This completely fails to meet the marginal requirement ceiling of $\le 400$\,ms.
    \item \textbf{Accelerated Configuration (GPU-enabled):} Reduced the segmentation execution phase down to a stable mean of 38\,ms. This dropped the mean total end-to-end processing pipeline time to \textbf{266\,ms}, satisfying the requirement in expectation. However, deterministic worst-case testing revealed a peak latency spike of \textbf{449\,ms}, which breaches the MAR threshold during transient computational loads.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{0.2_Pictures/tests/test1/latency_waterfall.png}
    \caption{Computational latency profiles within the software execution framework. The left chart displays a comparative breakdown between the baseline CPU and accelerated GPU pipelines against the targeted MAR (400\,ms) and IDE (100\,ms) thresholds. The right chart displays the variability across 100 runs.}
    \label{fig:latency_waterfall}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Intent Precision and Multi-View System Gain (Req. 2.7)}
The baseline grasp types are compared to the calculated grasps for both the multi-view and single-view configurations. As displayed in \autoref{fig:accuracy_gain}, the multi-view system demonstrates a positive centralised accuracy shift, yielding a $+1.78$ percentage point increase in mean intent accuracy over single-view tracking. However, the distribution exhibits substantial geometric variance, featuring a negative performance tail that reaches a maximum degradation of $-10\%$ for complex object contours. This high variability indicates that while sensor fusion provides a systemic advantage on average, a global performance dominance cannot be deterministically guaranteed across all individual trials.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

    \caption{Statistical distribution of autonomous grasp type prediction accuracy gains achieved via the multi-view system over standard single-view inputs.}
    \label{fig:accuracy_gain}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Sampling Density vs. Grasp Quality Impact (Req. 1.6 \& 2.7)}
To investigate the relationship between sample counts for the grasp prediction and tracking performance, the mean combined grasp score was mapped against prediction samples as illustrated in \autoref{fig:score_vs_samples}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-View:} Multi-view tracking consistently outperforms single-view tracking across all tested sample sizes. This margin expands as sample counts grow, confirming that higher sample counts allow the grasp prediction to search for better grasp hypotheses.
    \item \textbf{Baseline Gap:} Both configurations scale from $10^3$ toward $10^5$ samples. However, a visible difference remains relative to the unoccluded, high-fidelity Baseline ($\text{mean} = 0.885$). This gap highlights the persistent challenge of real-time viewpoint occlusion, which limits performance even at higher sample densities.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Requirement 1.6: Autonomy}
\begin{quote}
    \textit{The prosthesis shall autonomously compute viable grasp commands}
\end{quote}
The pipeline successfully satisfies this functional requirement by autonomously transforming fused, multi-view point cloud data into execution-ready grasp commands and wrist poses across the entire synthetic object library.

\subsubsection{Requirement 2.4: Pipeline Latency}
\begin{quote}
    \textit{Maximum computation time from EMG signal to autonomous motor command output should marginally be $\le 400$\,ms and ideally be $\le 100$\,ms}
\end{quote}
The system's expected latency clears the marginal value, but not the ideal value. Its compliance is strictly dependent upon the use of GPU acceleration. When executed on standard CPU architectures, the segmentation bottleneck causes a complete system latency failure of 955\,ms. Transitioning the workload to an accelerated GPU backend successfully drops the mean end-to-end processing latency to \textbf{266\,ms}, though
spikes reaching up to \textbf{449\,ms} remain an operational limitation.

\subsubsection{Requirement 2.7: Intent Precision}
\begin{quote}
    \textit{$\Delta$ percentage of correct autonomous grasp type and wrist pose predictions (MVSS vs. single-view) should marginally be $> 0$\,\% and ideally be $\ge 10$\,\%}
\end{quote}
The Multi-View Sensing System (MVSS) satisfies the baseline requirement by generating an overall positive tracking advancement over standalone single-view configurations. However, due to the stochastic nature of the sampler, there is high variance in the underlying grasp predictions. This architectural noise prevents the multi-view framework from outperforming single-view approaches in every individual trial instance; nevertheless, the aggregate distribution confirms a clear cumulative edge highlighted by a $+1.78\%$ mean accuracy gain across the 9 synthetic objects.
